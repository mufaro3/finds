\renewcommand{\maketitlehookd}{
  \begin{abstract}
    \noindent
    Animals travelling in cohesive groups yields many collective and individual
    benefits, such as security against predators and energy efficiency. The
    replication of such benefits for analogous, biology-inspired technologies
    like autonomous drone systems requires keen understanding of the cohesion
    and evolution of systems of these groups, but these investigations are
    fundamentally limited to small sizes due to the high computational
    complexity in computing pairwise dynamics at order $N^2$.
    As real schools of fish have been recorded to contain millions of members,
    FINDS, or Fish INteraction and Dynamics Simulator, was created to
    efficiently simulate the dynamics of very large schools of fish using
    common computational techniques employed in $N$-body simulation. FINDS is
    implemented as a containerized, modular software suite written
    predominantly in C (with Python for post-processing) that handles three
    core simulation processes in order. First, FINDS contains routines for
    computing the time-derivative of a school, which is a function of the sum
    of the pairwise interactions for each member of the school. As such, FINDS
    contains implementations of Brute Force computation, the Barnes-Hut
    algorithm, and the Fast Multipole Method. Second, FINDS contains custom
    routines for numerically integrating these systems using adaptive and
    non-adaptive Runge-Kutta schemes for adaptive time-stepping. Third,
    FINDS contains a small library of post-processing modules written in Python
    for stream-lining the analysis of generated data.
  \end{abstract}
}

\section{Mathematical Model of Swimmers}

FINDS is developed around the far-field source/sink dipole model designed by
Mabrouk and Floryan for modelling how passive hydrodynamic interactions at far
distances effects the evolution of a greater school
\cite{mabrouk2024,mabrouk2025}.
Under this model, each fish $i$ is defined as a dipole consisting of a
fluid source at the head of the fish $\vu{x}_{f,i}$, and a fluid sink at the
tail $\vu{x}_{b,i}$, separated by the length of the fish $\ell_i$. The
source and the sink of each fish are defined to have the same volumetric flow
rate, $\sigma_i$, to ensure the conservation of fluid by making the sinks
consume exactly the same amount of fluid as the sources. For simplicity, the
source and sink (or ``front'' and ``back'') of each fish are called its
``features.''

Each fish is defined as an eight-dimensional vector consisting of four
variables: $\va{x}_{c,i}$, the three-dimensional center-of-mass position vector,
$\vu{n}_{i}$, the three-dimensional orientation unit vector, $\ell_i$, and
$\sigma_i$. Together, the collection of each of these fish vectors at a given
time forms the state vector $\va{X}$.

In particular, $\va{x}_{c,i}$ and $\vu{n}_{i}$ are dynamic and $\ell_i$ and
$\sigma_i$ are constant for each fish $i$. This formulism leaves the
translational and rotational dynamics for each fish as the system of two
coupled first-order differential equations concerning $\va{x}_{c,i}$ and
$\vu{n}_{i}$ respectively. With this, the derivatives of each are defined as
\begin{align}\label{eq-dynamics}
  \dv{\va{x}_{c,i}}{t} &= \frac{1}{2}\left(\dv{\va{x}_{f,i}}{t} +
                          \dv{\va{x}_{b,i}}{t}\right) \\
  \dv{\vu{n}_{i}}{t}   &= \frac{\Delta \va{v}_{i} +
                          2 \lambda_i \vu{n}_i}{\ell_i}
\end{align}

\noindent
where

\begin{equation}\label{eq-lagrange-multiplier}
  \lambda_i = \frac{\Delta \va{v}_i \vdot \vu{n}_i}{2}
\end{equation}

\noindent
is a Lagrange multiplier to ensure that $\ell_i$ is kept constant, and

\begin{equation}\label{eq-velocity-diff}
  \Delta \va{v}_i = \dv{\va{x}_{f,i}}{t} - \dv{\va{x}_{b,i}}{t}.
\end{equation}

With this, the translational and rotational dynamics for each fish are variably
dependent on the translational derivatives of the source and sink for each fish,
and this reduces the problem of modelling fish to an analogous problem of simple
Laplacian particle dynamics based on treating the sources and sinks as
individual particles with charge $\sigma_i$. Under this lens, the
potential function resulting from a particle is the charge multiplied by
Green's function for the three-dimensional Laplace equation,

\begin{equation}\label{eq-greens}
  \Phi_i(r) = \sigma_i G(r) = -\frac{\sigma_i}{4\pi r}.
\end{equation}

However, as this model is designed for far-field interactions, it becomes
unphysical for small distances, as infinitely small distances produce
infinitely large potential. To solve this problem, Mabrouk and Floryan
reformulated this potential with a regularization parameter $\varepsilon$
defined as

\begin{equation}\label{eq-greens-regularized}
  \Phi_{i,\varepsilon}(r) = \Phi_{i}(r) \erf(r/\varepsilon).
\end{equation}

From the potential functions, the velocity field resulting from any source or
sink is defined as the gradient of the potential function, and for simplicity,
the displacement-dependent vector $\va{k}$ can be defined to represent the
gradient of the Laplacian interaction kernel $1/r$,

\begin{align}\label{eqs-interaction}
  \va{k}(\va{r}) &= \grad (-\frac{1}{r}) = \frac{\va{r}}{r^3} \\
  \va{k}_{\varepsilon}(\va{r}) &=
  \grad\left(-\frac{\erf(r/\varepsilon)}{r}\right) =
  \va{k}(\va{r}) \xi_{\varepsilon}(r)
\end{align}

\noindent
where $\xi_{i,\varepsilon}(r)$ is the regularization coefficient

\begin{equation}\label{eq-regularization}
  \xi_{\varepsilon}(r) = \erf(r/\epsilon) -
  \frac{2r}{\varepsilon \sqrt{\pi}} \exp(-\frac{r^2}{\varepsilon^2}).
\end{equation}

\noindent
The velocity fields are therefore

\begin{align}\label{eqs-velocity-field}
  \va{u}_{i}(\va{r}) = \grad \Phi_i &= \frac{\sigma_i}{4\pi} \va{k}(\va{r}) \\
  \va{u}_{i,\varepsilon}(\va{r}) = \grad \Phi_{i,\varepsilon} =
  \frac{\sigma_i}{4\pi} \va{k}_{\varepsilon}(\va{r})
\end{align}

\noindent
for the unregularized and regularized velocity fields respectively.

With the velocity fields well-defined, the velocity of a given source or sink
$\alpha$ can be defined generally as

\begin{equation}\label{eq-feature-velocity}
  \dv{\va{x}_{\alpha,i}}{t} = U_i \vu{n}_i +
  \sum_{i \neq j}^N \frac{\sigma_j}{4\pi} \va{K}_{\alpha,ij}.
\end{equation}

\noindent
 where the first term is the \textit{internal} velocity contribution and the
second term is the \textit{external} velocity contribution. In detail,

\begin{equation}\label{eq-internal-velocity-contrib}
  U_i = \frac{\sigma_i}{4\pi \ell_i^2}
\end{equation}

\noindent
is the self-propelled speed resulting from the interaction between any
source-sink pair of a dipole, and the second term represents the sum of the
velocity contribution to a source or sink from a dipole, where
$\va{K}_{\alpha,ij}$ represents the composite interaction vector between feature
$\alpha$ of fish $i$ with both the source $f$ and the sink $b$ of fish $j$,

\begin{equation}\label{eq-composite-interaction}
  \va{K}_{\alpha,ij} = \va{k}(\va{r}_{\alpha f}) - \va{k}(\va{r}_{\alpha b}).
\end{equation}

\noindent
where $\va{r}_{\alpha \beta}$ is the displacement between feature $\alpha$ of
fish $i$ and feature $\beta$ of fish $j$. In the case of regularization, the
interaction function $\va{k}$ is simply replaced with $\va{k}_\varepsilon$.

\section{Derivative Computation}

As stated previously, the modelling of fish is an $N$-body problem where the
algorithmic complexity of computing a derivative is, through Brute-Force
calculations, of order $\O(N^2)$. To reduce this computation time,
two tree-based approximation algorithms are implemented: the Barnes-Hut
algorithm and the Fast Multipole Method, of orders $\O(N \log N)$ and
$\O(N)$ respectively.

Brute force computation and the Barnes-Hut approximation are implemented
directly into the FINDS code-base, and as such, the user has the ability to
switch between unregularized and regularized calculations with ease. However,
the Fast-Multipole Method is computed externally using Greengard et al.'s Fast
Multipole Method library FMM3D, which will only compute the standard Laplacian
gradient $\va{u}_i$, so the Fast-Multipole Method cannot be used with
regularization. Additionally, all three methods are CPU-parallelized using
OpenMP.
